# Twain: habits in these four pieces

- Opening: a flat claim or a personal confession in the first sentence, no scene-setting. `As concerns tobacco, there are many superstitions.` / `I do not claim that I can tell a story as it ought to be told.`
- Paragraph to paragraph: plain additive steps, one item per paragraph, often counted off. `The next superstition is that a man has a standard of his own.` / `However, there are only two best ways to travel through Switzerland.`
- Rhythm: short flat declaratives, then one long sentence that piles up detail, then a deadpan snap. `He thinks he has, but he hasn’t.` / `Me, who never learned to smoke, but always smoked; me, who came into the world asking for a light.`
- Facts and numbers: exact, specific, used for comedy rather than proof. `cigars which cost him forty cents apiece and bore red-and-gold labels` / `she has fifty thousand children; of these, about one hundred are sons`
- Reader: speaks as "I" with a named acquaintance or a personal anecdote; "you" appears as a casual aside. `but you can glide smoothly in a carriage in ten` / `understand, I mean by word of mouth, not print`
- Images: nature and places described in business, politics or clothing terms. `the board of directors will dethrone her` / `a ladder railroad or two up its back like suspenders`
- Never explains the joke or softens the claim; he says outright that spelling out the point is the mistake. `he shouts it at you--every time` / `All of which is very depressing, and makes one want to renounce joking and lead a better life.`
- Ends: on the last beat of the anecdote or a one-line deferral, with no moral drawn. `He told me afterward that some day I would get shot for giving people that kind of cigars to smoke.`
